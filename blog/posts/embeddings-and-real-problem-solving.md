I once solved an embeddings interview question that ended up becoming something bigger than a single answer. A colleague started using it to measure something specific: **how quickly a person can genuinely understand a technical problem and start tackling it — without reaching for AI.** It got me thinking about what we're really testing when we hire, and where AI actually fits.

## The question, and why it stuck

The problem was about embeddings — representing text as vectors so a machine can measure how *similar* two pieces of meaning are. What made it a good question wasn't the code. It was watching *how* someone breaks it down: Do they understand why embeddings work? Can they reason about similarity, dimensionality, and edge cases before writing a single line?

My colleague realized it was a clean signal for one thing that's hard to fake — **how long it takes someone to understand a problem and begin solving it on their own.**

## Why "without AI" matters here

This isn't anti-AI. It's about what the exercise reveals. If a solution only appears because someone prompted a model, you learn very little about whether they actually understand the problem. And understanding is exactly the part that *doesn't* transfer to the tool:

- AI can write the function — but it can't decide the function is the wrong approach.
- AI can suggest an embedding model — but it can't tell you your data makes it the wrong choice.
- AI is confident even when it's wrong — only understanding catches that.

Fundamentals are what let you *direct* the tool instead of being led by it.

## But the goal isn't to avoid AI — it's to aim it

Here's the other half, and it matters just as much. Once you understand the problem, AI is a force multiplier. The strongest engineers I know don't refuse AI to prove a point — they use it to move faster on the parts that don't need deep thought, so they can spend their judgment where it actually counts.

The real skill isn't "can you code without AI." It's:

1. **Understand the business problem** deeply enough to frame it correctly.
2. **Know the fundamentals** well enough to choose the right approach.
3. **Use AI fluently** to reach a working solution faster.

Take away steps 1 and 2, and AI just helps you build the wrong thing quickly.

## The takeaway

That embeddings question measured something durable: can you understand a problem and move toward a solution under your own power? That's the foundation. AI sits on top of it — a powerful tool in the hands of someone who knows what they're really trying to solve.

> Fundamentals tell you *what* to build. AI helps you build it faster. The best work needs both.
